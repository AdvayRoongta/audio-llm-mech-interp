The behavioural results show that when a text claim contradicts the voice, the model follows the text almost every time, with a text-dominance rate of 0.983 for emotional prosody and 0.933 for speaker identity. Text-dominance rate here means the fraction of conflict items on which the model's answer matches the false text claim rather than what the audio actually contains. This section asks why that suppression of paralinguistic information happens, and whether it can be undone. We work through four questions in order: whether the audio information reaches the model at all, where in the network the decision is made, whether the repairs used in prior studies work here, and, since they do not, what does.

The simplest explanation would be that the audio encoder discards prosody before the language model ever sees it, so that the model follows the text because the text is all it has. We can rule this out. To find what information is present inside the network, we use a probe, which is a small linear classifier trained on the model's own internal activations while the model itself stays frozen. If a simple classifier can read a property off those activations, that property is present in a form the model could use, and probing layer by layer shows where it appears and whether it survives. Speaker gender is recoverable at 1.000 accuracy against a chance rate of 0.5 from encoder layer 13 onward, and, importantly, it is still at 1.000 after the projector, the module that converts the audio encoder's output into the representation the language model actually reads. The signal is not lost in transit, so whatever goes wrong happens after this point, inside the language model itself.

Two separate controls make that number trustworthy. First, we split the data by actor, so that no speaker appears in both the training and test sets. Without this, a probe could score well simply by memorising individual voices rather than learning anything about gender. Second, we retrain the probe on randomly shuffled labels, which yields 0.43, close to chance as it should be. This second control does a different job from the first: it confirms the probe is not merely fitting noise in high-dimensional activations.

A probe shows that the information is readable, but not that the model uses it. For that we need a causal test, so we delete the text claim from the input and ask the same questions again. With the claim present, the model answers from the audio on 0.000 of prosody items and 0.067 of identity items. With the claim removed, those rise to 0.533 and 0.717, against chance rates of 0.125 for the eight emotion options and 0.5 for the two gender options. The model, the audio, and the questions are identical across the two conditions, and the only difference is one sentence of text. The model can do this task from audio and does not do so when text is available.

To find the components responsible, we use attribution patching, a method that estimates how much each part of the network contributes to the final decision. The exact version of this measurement would re-run the model once for every component, replacing that component each time, which is far too slow for a network of this size. Attribution patching approximates the same quantity using gradients, requiring only one forward and one backward pass. Because it is an approximation it needs verification, which we do with a faithfulness check: we keep only the top twenty percent of components that the method ranked highest and test whether they reproduce what the full model does. They do, at 1.00, so the small set we identified really is the machinery driving the behaviour rather than an artifact of the approximation. We group positions in the input by their role, separating the audio tokens, the text claim, the question, the answer options, and the position at which the model produces its answer, where a position means a slot in the input sequence, since the model reads text and audio as a sequence of tokens and computes its answer at the final one. The competition between the two modalities happens at that answer position, and it is heavily lopsided: text contributes 3.29 against audio's 0.18, a ratio of roughly eighteen to one. This tells us where to intervene, but not yet how.

Our first attempt followed Cho et al., who studied conflicts between two text sources and found that back-patching reduced the dominance of one source over the other. Back-patching means taking the activations that a late layer has computed and copying them back into an early layer, then re-running the rest of the network, which in effect gives the evidence a second pass through the model's reasoning and more opportunity to influence the outcome. In our setting it did nothing at all. Audio accuracy remained at 0.000 across every source layer, destination layer, and window width we tried. A mid-layer source performed no worse than a late one and every configuration sat at the floor, which means there was no late-to-early mechanism being exploited in the first place. The method was not being misapplied; there was simply nothing there for it to move.

We then asked a blunter question, which is whether the audio evidence could be made stronger. We swept which layer we copy from, how many layers we write into, and how much we amplify the copied activations. To detect small effects we need something finer than accuracy, which was pinned at zero and could not show partial movement, so we use the decision margin, defined as the difference in log-probability that the model assigns to the audio-correct answer versus the text-claimed answer, measured in nats. A negative margin means the model favours the text, and at baseline this margin is -7.32, a very large preference. Everything we tried moved it slightly and none of it moved it enough. The best single source layer, layer 16, gained 0.51 nats, under a tenth of the gap, while sources in the late layers made matters worse, falling as low as -0.38. Widening the destination window helped less rather than more. Amplification saturated at roughly 0.10, and at the highest gain the model broke outright, with numerically unstable output and its ability to do the task at all collapsing to 0.100. The conclusion is that this cannot be fixed from the audio side. The audio is not losing because it is too quiet, so making it louder does not change the outcome.

Since the audio side was a dead end, we intervened on the text side using attention knockout. The model's attention mechanism lets each position read from other positions, and a specific connection can be severed by masking it, after which we observe whether the behaviour changes. If severing a connection changes nothing, that connection was not carrying the information. We blocked the answer position from attending to the claim tokens, and this also did nothing: blocking that connection at all thirty-two layers left the decision unchanged, closing none of the gap. The reason turns out to be informative. By the time the model produces an answer, the claim is no longer only in the claim tokens, because it has already been copied into the words that sit between the claim and the answer. We tested this by varying which positions lose sight of the claim while holding everything else fixed. Blocking only the answer position closed none of the gap, blocking every position after the claim closed all of it, and blocking every position closed all of it as well, with the second and third conditions producing identical results in both categories. The claim is therefore absorbed early into the question and option tokens, and the answer position reads it from them. Cutting the direct connection changes nothing because the information travels by a detour. This explains why the obvious intervention fails, and it is the central mechanistic finding of this work.

Everything described so far measures multiple-choice answers, in which the model picks a letter, and that turned out to be the wrong place to look. When the model writes a description instead of picking an option, the same intervention has a large effect, provided it is applied in the middle layers. We prompt it to describe the speaker in one or two sentences, leave the false claim in the prompt, and record which label the description actually names. Without intervention the model named the false claimed label in every description, in both categories. With the mid-layer block it named the false label in 0.375 of prosody descriptions, with a ninety-five percent interval of 0.21 to 0.57, and in 0.433 of identity descriptions, with an interval of 0.27 to 0.61. The true label, which the model never named at baseline in either category, was named in 0.500 of prosody descriptions and 0.567 of identity descriptions. The baseline intervals are 0.86 to 1.00 and 0.89 to 1.00, so they do not overlap the intervened condition in either case.

The generated text does not degrade under this intervention; it improves. Scored by the unmodified model, mean per-token log-probability rises from -1.28 to -0.64 for prosody and from -1.45 to -0.78 for identity, repetition falls, and length is essentially unchanged. In practice the output moves from descriptions such as "The speaker is an English-speaking female who sounds happy" to "The speaker sounds surprised while stating that kids are talking by the door", where surprised is what the voice actually conveys and happy was the false claim. One methodological point matters here. We score the generated text in a context from which the claim has been removed, because scoring it against a prompt that still contains the claim would judge any text correctly contradicting that claim as improbable, and a working intervention would then appear to be producing nonsense. The measurement has to be blind to agreement with the claim, or it measures the wrong thing.

Two differences explain why generation moves while multiple choice does not. In multiple-choice scoring only a single position is attenuated, whereas during generation every token the model produces is blocked from the claim. In addition, the description prompt contains no list of options, so far less intervening text is available to carry the claim forward, and it is precisely that intervening text which the routing result identified as the actual route.

Four controls rule out specific alternative explanations. The first is that the intervention did not damage the model: on matched items, where the claim agrees with the audio, accuracy rises rather than falls, from 0.767 to 0.900 for prosody and from 0.933 to 1.000 for identity, which is the opposite of what a broken model would produce. The second, and the most important, is that the intervention is not simply making the model ignore all text. On text-only items, where there is no audio at all and the claim is the only evidence available, accuracy is unchanged, at 0.567 before and after for prosody and 1.000 before and after for identity. We reduce the claim's influence rather than removing it, so the outcome only changes when there is competing evidence for it to change to, and when the claim is all the model has, it still uses it. The third control shows the effect is not generic damage from perturbing attention, since applying the identical block to the late layers has no effect whatsoever, leaving the model repeating the false claim in every description in both categories despite the same amount of interference. The fourth shows the intervention is not equivalent to hiding the claim from the model, because applying the block at every layer suppresses the claim more but costs capability, dropping to 0.667 on matched prosody items and falling below the 0.767 baseline. More suppression is not more benefit, and the effect is specific to a range of depths, which is not what deletion of the input by degrees would produce.

We attempted this analysis on a second model, SALMONN-13B, and excluded it under the same capability criterion we apply throughout. That criterion asks whether a model can answer from audio alone before any conflict result may be read as suppression, and SALMONN does not clear it in substance. It answers emotional prosody questions from audio at 0.188 against a chance rate of 0.125, a margin thin enough to rest on the sample, and on the subset used for the intervention it sits at 0.133, which is chance. On speaker identity it is below chance, at 0.453 against 0.500. A model that cannot perform the task from audio cannot be shown to be suppressing audio, and there is correspondingly nothing for our intervention to restore; applied regardless, it degrades the model rather than helping it. One caveat applies to this exclusion and should be stated plainly. Our screen measures whether a model answers correctly from audio in our prompt format, and it cannot separate a model that does not perceive prosody from one that perceives it but does not answer in the form we ask for. SALMONN returns a response outside the option set on 0.203 of items, where Qwen never does, and its encoder demonstrably carries the signal, recoverable at 0.671 after the projector against a chance rate of 0.143. Part of its low score is therefore likely to be instruction-following rather than perception. We treat the exclusion as a statement about what we are able to measure in this model rather than as a claim about what it can hear, and we report the attempt rather than omitting it, since the criterion is the one that already excludes a model from our behavioural results and was fixed before the outcome was known.

Our dataset defines a third conflict category, sarcasm and pacing, which we ran but do not report as a result. Its audio comes from MUStARD++, and a substantial share of those clips are identified by episode ids the public release does not distribute, leaving 463 of 798 audio-bearing items, which is still more than the analysis requires. What the category cannot supply is a clean answer. Our method assumes the audio determines a well-defined correct label, against which siding with the text can be counted as an error, and that assumption holds comfortably for emotion and for speaker gender but not here. Removing the text claim raises audio accuracy only to 0.233 against a chance rate of 0.100, and on matched items the model's accuracy without the claim is 0.167, which is close to chance, so there is very little audio-grounded performance for an intervention to recover. The clearest symptom is in the descriptions themselves: only ten of thirty named a single identifiable label, with the rest naming several or none. Part of this is the label set. The category asks for one of ten emotional tones over television dialogue, and several of those options, ridicule and frustration among them, are social judgments about what was said and to whom rather than properties a listener can recover from the voice alone, which is a harder question than the eight-way choice over acted studio speech we use for prosody or the two-way choice for speaker gender. On that remainder the intervention's confidence intervals overlap the baseline entirely, so we treat the category as underpowered rather than as a negative result. Two further caveats apply to any future attempt: the missing clips are not a random subset, and they fall most heavily on the mismatched condition that carries the phenomenon, retaining 140 of 266 against 168 of 266 for matched items. We regard sarcasm as the most immediate gap in this work, but closing it needs a label set the audio can actually settle, not only the missing recordings.

Several other limits should be read literally. The intervention is unconditional, in that we reduce attention to the claim whether or not the claim is true, and the model has not learned to judge the conflict. It works because reducing a signal only changes an outcome when something else is competing, and we measure that cost directly and find it to be zero on the items where the claim is the only evidence. We have not tested the harder case of genuinely ambiguous audio paired with a reliable text claim, and we do not claim to have fixed multimodal conflict resolution. The effect appears in open generation and not in multiple-choice answering, where it closes none of the decision gap for prosody and one percent for identity. Since these models are typically used by asking them to describe what they hear, we take generation to be the setting of interest, but the limitation is a real one. The positive result comes from a single model, so the mechanism is demonstrated rather than shown to be general, and the second model we tested establishes a precondition for it rather than confirming it. The method assumes that the claim's token span is known, which holds by construction in our data but would need to be solved in deployment. Finally, a partial block, which reduces attention to the claim to a quarter rather than removing it, had no effect at any depth, so the intervention behaves as a switch rather than a dial and we do not describe it as graded.

No method in this section is new. Probing follows Pasad et al., attribution patching follows AtP*, attention knockout follows Geva et al., and circuit localisation and back-patching follow Cho et al., whose repair result we reproduce here as a negative in a new setting. What is our own is the set of controls: verifying that the knockout is connected before trusting a null result, scoring generated text in a context free of the claim, measuring which label a free-form description names, and the matched and text-only controls described above.
